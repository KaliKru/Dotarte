Schreibheft is a handwriting notebook for German learners at B2–C1 level: they write by hand (or type) and only then see their own mistakes, explained the way a teacher would. From idea to a working product in one day, built with an AI assistant.

    Where it started

    While I was preparing for telc C1, I noticed the same thing in myself and in my classmates: writing was the hardest part, even though we could all speak and understand German well. Everyone had developed their own strategies to deal with it.

    I found my own workaround. I wanted to write by hand, but a paper notebook can’t tell me what I got wrong, the way autocorrect does on a computer. So I wrote on my iPad with Apple Pencil, let iOS turn my handwriting into typed text, and then checked whether it was correct.

    Schreibheft turns this workaround into one app. Together with an AI assistant, I wanted to find out quickly whether the idea could work: first for myself, but designed with other learners in mind.

    The problem

    Many German learners at B2 and C1 level speak fluently, but they struggle to write correctly.

    • Exams are written on paper. Certificates like telc are taken with a paper answer sheet. In telc Deutsch C1 Hochschule, for example, candidates have 70 minutes to write a text of about 350 words. There is no spellcheck to lean on.
    • Everyday tools hide mistakes. Autocorrect and spellcheck fix errors silently, so learners never see what they got wrong, and the same mistakes come back in the exam.
    • Feedback is hard to get. A teacher who corrects texts is expensive, and generic grammar checkers rewrite sentences instead of explaining them.

    Why handwriting

    Research suggests that writing by hand engages the brain more fully than typing, which can support learning.

    • More brain connectivity. In a 2024 EEG study, Van der Weel and Van der Meer recorded 36 university students writing words with a digital pen and typing them. Handwriting produced much more widespread connectivity between brain regions linked to memory and learning. The participants wrote on a touchscreen with a digital pen, much like Apple Pencil on an iPad.

      Note: the study measured brain activity, not test results.

    • Faster learning in a new language. In a Johns Hopkins study (Wiley & Rapp, Psychological Science, 2021), 42 adults learned the Arabic alphabet by writing, typing or watching videos. The handwriting group learned faster and did better at writing, spelling and reading new words. The authors advise adult language learners to add “good old-fashioned paperwork” to their app study.

      Note: the study looked at learning letters, not writing full sentences.

    Together, the two studies support the idea behind Schreibheft: practise writing by hand, and see your own mistakes.

    The idea

    Schreibheft is a writing notebook where learners write their own sentence, by hand with Apple Pencil or by typing, and nothing is corrected until they tap “Prüfen”. Autocorrect is switched off, and the handwriting recognition is told to keep every mistake instead of guessing what the learner meant.

    Key design decisions

    Think first, then reveal.

    Mistakes are only marked at first. Each one gets a small hint (“Tipp: Endung”) before the solution, and learners open solutions one by one.

    Teacher-style feedback.

    The correction appears in red handwriting right above the learner’s own words, with a short explanation in plain language and no grammar jargon.

    Four clear error types.

    Rechtschreibung, Grammatik, Wortstellung and Ausdruck. Each has its own colour, so patterns become visible.

    Swipeable summary cards.

    The corrected sentence, a more natural version, a more formal version for letters and requests, and tips for next time.

    Light motivation.

    A daily goal, a streak, and 70 writing prompts in 7 topics, including formal letters as practised for exams.

    Built for the iPad first.

    One floating toolbar, hints that point to the button they explain, and layouts that adapt to phone, tablet and desktop.

    Process

    I built Schreibheft together with an AI assistant (Claude), which took me from idea to a working, testable product within one day.

    1. Test the riskiest part first. A quick prototype answered one question: can handwriting recognition keep a learner’s mistakes instead of correcting them?
    2. Short feedback loops. I used the app on my iPad, spotted what felt wrong (double markings, unclear explanations, hints in the wrong place) and refined it straight away.
    3. Three visual directions. I compared mockups of a calm, minimal version, a friendly, lively version and a hybrid. I chose the hybrid: lively visuals with a calm, focused writing screen and one floating toolbar.
    4. Check on every device. Real screenshots on phone and iPad, upright and sideways, showed layout problems early.

    Working with AI let me spend my time on design and learning decisions, while the assistant handled the implementation.

    What I learned

    1

    AI needs guardrails, not just prompts.

    The AI sometimes marked the same mistake twice, or called a capitalization error “word order”. Better instructions helped, but the real fix was a check in the app itself: do the corrections add up to the corrected sentence? I learned that a good AI product needs a safety net for the moments the model is wrong.

    2

    Sometimes the best UX is slowing people down.

    Most apps try to give answers as fast as possible. For learning, the opposite works: show only where the mistake is, give a hint, and let the learner think before revealing the solution. Designing for “productive struggle” was a new way of thinking about UX for me.

    3

    Test on the real device, early and often.

    Many problems only showed up when I used the app on my iPad: the trash icon was unclear, a hint pointed to the wrong place, and the cards seemed to float without context. Screenshots on phone and tablet revealed layout issues I would never have noticed on my laptop.

    4

    AI speeds up building, but the decisions stay human.

    With an AI assistant I had a working product within one day. The faster the building got, the more my time went into judgment: what to show, what to hide, which of three design directions fits the users. AI changed where I spend my time, not who makes the decisions.
